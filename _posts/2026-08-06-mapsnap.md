OldNYC is a historical photo viewer that I built with the New York Public Library (NYPL). It shows you a map of the city with photos at nearly every intersection, NNN photos in all.

At the heart of OldNYC is _geocoding_: the process of going from text like "at the intersection of 10th Street and Avenue A" to a latitude and longitude for a pin on the map. OldNYC originally used the Google Maps Geocoder; now it uses OpenStreetMap (OSM).

But this has always been an anachronism: Google Maps and OSM map _today's_ streets, but the photos describe streets from around 1870-1950. Often these are the same: Broadway and 60th Street is in the same place today as it was 100 years go. But there are a few cases where they aren't:

- The photo describes an intersection that no longer exists, either because the street has been renamed or the street was removed in one of the many urban renewal projects of the 20th century.
- The photo mentions an address ("1234 Broadway"), and addresses are sometimes renumbered.

The ultimate answer to both of these is a _historic map_, and the ultimate historic map for cities in North America in this time period is the Sanborn Insurance Maps.

The Sanborn Insurance Map company produced detailed, block-by-block maps of every city in North America from 1867 to around 1960. Here's what one page of one map looks like:

Buildings are outlined with their actual shapes. The color indicates building material (red is brick, blue is stone, brown is fireproof). The buildings are labeled with their occupants (often a business, or D for Dwelling). Since these are fire insurance maps, the fire hydrants and water pipes are carefully labeled. And, important for us, the street addresses are labeled in front of each building!

This is one page of one Sanborn map. There are around a _million_ of these pages in existence. Many of them are in the public domain, have been scanned by the Library of Congress, and are freely available.

(for more on Sanborn, see this video and this writeup)

One problem with the Sanborn maps is that it can be hard to find the exact page you're interested in. The Sanborn Map Company divided Brooklyn into sixteen "volumes," so you need to figure out which one you're in. Then you need to locate the page of interest, find it on the Library of Congress's web site, orient yourself, and find the building you're interested in.

This reflects the limitations of printed maps, limitations that web-based maps don't share. This would all be easier if we could render the Sanborn pages on a modern map like Google Maps or MapLibre.

To do this, you have to _georeference_ the map. This means lining it up with a web map and, in practice, this is done by locating a few points on the Sanborn page on a web map. Two or three is usually sufficient.

There's a whole web community devoted to doing exactly this: OldInsuranceMaps.net (OIM), run by Adam Cox. OIM features a web interface for analyzing and georeferencing Sanborn maps, then browsing them on a web map. The OIM community has georeferenced thousands of maps since the site started in 2022.

Still, there are a lot more maps that haven't been georeferenced, including almost all of New York City. I georeferenced one volume of the Brooklyn map (63 pages) by hand. On the one hand, it's interesting: you get to look at historic maps, and you're making clear, tangible progress. But it's also quite tedious, and I had a sneaking suspicion that I could get the computer to do this all for me.

That's largely turned out to be true, but the problem turned out to be much harder and more intersting than I expected.

Enter: mapsnap!

Mapsnap is a tool for automatically georeferencing Sanborn maps. To use it, you provide two things:

1. A manifest file from the Library of Congress for your Sanborn volume
2. An OpenStreetMap relation that contains the map (its county, say)

From there, mapsnap downloads all the map pages and georeferences them. The end result is a IIIF file that you can view on Allmaps or any other GIS program. You can take a look at the 1939 Brooklyn volume here.

How does this work?

Models

To understand how Mapsnap works, it helps to understand how humans georeference a map on OIM. OIM shows you a Sanborn page and a MapLibre map. You click a point on the Sanborn map (often an intersection) and then the matching point on the map. This is called a Ground Control Point (GCP).

After you've produced three GCPs, OIM has enough information to produce a "fit." This is a transformation from the image's pixel coordinates to latitudes and longitudes. This is a linear model (aka "affine" or "polynomial1" in GIS speak) with six parameters.

Four are straightforward:

- Translation: where in the world is this page? (two variables, lat & lng)
- Scale: how large is the map, in pixels per foot?
- Rotation: which direction is north? Sanborn maps are rotated every which way.

The other two are more subtle:

- Skew: Are the vertical and horizontal directions on the page actually 90° from one another?
- Scale anisotropy: is a pixel on the page's x-axis the same distance as a pixel on the y-axis?

For a well-made map, there should be no skew and no scale anisotropy. And the Sanborn maps are very well made! If your GCPs yield a fit with skew or scale anisotropy, you've almost certainly done something wrong.

Mapsnap bakes this idea in: it assumes zero skew and zero scale anisotropy. This yields a four parameter model, not six, and it means we only need two GCPs, not three. In GIS speak, this is called a "Helmert" fit.

So mapsnap's goal is to find two points in each Sanborn image that it can associate with points on the map. In practice these are always street intersections.

Reading the text

The first step is OCR: getting the computer to read all those street labels in the Sanborn image. This winds up being a pretty interesting variant on your garden variety OCR:

- We have a restricted vocabulary: just the names of streets in our area of interest.
- Street labels can be horizontal (for left-right streets), vertical, or any other angle for a diagonal street.
- There's a _ton_ of text in every image that we don't care about: address numbers, business names, descriptions of water mains.
- There's a lot of noise: water pipes, fire hydrants and address numbers all appear very close to the streets.

(CLARK and MYRTLE are good examples here)

In addition, there's semantic ambiguity: the image clearly says "BROOKLYN BRIDGE APPROACH." There's a Brooklyn Ave in Brooklyn. And there's a Bridge Street. But this doesn't refer to either of those.

I wound up using EasyOCR, a model from 2020. As its name implies, it was pretty easy to set up! But crucially, it also proved simple enough to adapt to my needs.

EasyOCR has two parts:

- Detection: Given an image, which parts are text? EasyOCR uses the CRAFT detector, which was state of the art in 2019. For each page, it outputs a list of (rotated) boxes that might contain text.
- Recognition: In each box, try to read the text. EasyOCR uses a custom CRNN for this.

I didn't have to touch CRAFT much, but I heavily modified the recognition step:

1. It's only allowed to output street names that exist in the region of interest. If the text isn't a street name, it will come out as a random street name with very low confidence. This works much better than trying to post-process EasyOCR's text output.
2. I retrained EasyOCR's model with extra synthetic training data containing noise and typography typical of Sanborn maps. This includes water pipes and Sanborn's style of ordinals (1ST, 2ND, etc.).

The end result of this is a list of candidate street detections. For each of these, we have a position and an angle (the direction of the text).

From detections to a fit

There are a few ways to go from street detections to a fit, but one of the simplest is to try and find intersections. This is just like how you might produce a fit on OldInsuranceMaps.

Some of these are correct, but many are not. There are a few reasons an intersection might be in the wrong place:

- The streets might curve, in which case extrapolation is invalid.
- There might be a similarly-named street in another part of town: 3rd Street Northwest vs. 3rd Street Southwest in D.C., or 3rd Street vs. 3rd Avenue vs. 3rd Drive in Queens.
- The street might have been rerouted in the past 100 years.
- We might have detected something that isn't a street.
- We might have detected the street at a slightly off angle.
- We might have misread the street's name.

Ultimately, we need some way to distinguish the signal from the noise. The solution is RANSAC (Random Sample Consensus), a venerable algorithm from 1981. RANSAC is _robust_: you can add lots of noise without affecting it. Just like adding outliers to a data set doesn't affect the median, adding incorrect GCPs does not affect RANSAC. So long as the inaccurate GCPs are mutually consistent, it can actually tolerate >50% noise.

Here's how it works: we need two GCPs to produce a fit, so we try each pair of candidate GCPs in turn. Each pair produces a model. We take each street detection and run it through that model. Remember that a street detection has a position and an angle. The model turns that into a position and an angle on the map, i.e. a vector. If that vector is close to the street in OSM and at roughly the right angle, then we call it an "inlier." If not, it's an outlier. The fit is the pair of GCPs that produces the most inliers.
